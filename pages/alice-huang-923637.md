## 1. The claim about her origins (social-media story)

- A post circulating on the Chinese internet claims to show her junior-high graduation photo; she is identified as the fourth student from the left in the front row.
- Alleged background: working-class family; father worked at a machinery factory in Hunan; she attended the factory's middle school but never went to high school.
- Instead she went to a vocational health school (衛校), the type that trains nurses, then moved to Shenzhen during the reform era and worked as a hospital nurse.
- The post claims she "slept her way up" and became the hospital's Youth League leader; later moved to Beijing and became a journalist without evident journalism training.
- Former classmates describe her as street-smart, highly sociable, good with people — and able to hold her liquor, "an important social skill in China".
- Her class WeChat group was reportedly disbanded once she became internationally famous.
- The host says she finds the post credible because of its detail, and because Huang's education has always been conspicuously vague for someone so self-promoting. She also argues a Chinese vocational nursing qualification is not degree-level, unlike nursing in the US.
- **Caveat:** the host states plainly that "the above is a story from the internet" — none of it is confirmed reporting. Other outlets report she later took a master's in media studies at Hong Kong Baptist University and obtained Hong Kong permanent residency [1], which sits awkwardly with the "never finished high school" narrative.

## 2. Kang Wei — the financier who made her career

| Point | Detail given |
| --- | --- |
| Identity | Kang Wei (rendered "Conway"/"Khan" in the transcript), Shanghai financier, early 50s — about a decade older than Huang, who is in her 40s |
| Education | Shanghai University; began as a securities analyst at a large state-owned brokerage |
| Rise | Joined institutions affiliated with CNOOC (China National Offshore Oil Corporation); became general manager of Zhonghai Fund Management at 32, one of the youngest to run a major Chinese mutual fund |
| 2010 | Left to found his own private investment firm, Hexi Investment (上海和熙投资) |
| Relationship | Caixin and the *Financial Times* both describe a long personal and business partnership; Caixin, citing multiple sources, says they are husband and wife. When it began, and whether they are still married, is not established |

How Huang entered the picture:

- Before Hexi she worked in HSBC's private banking division in Hong Kong and later at a large Chinese securities firm (transcript garbles the name; probably CITIC Securities), in relatively junior roles.
- In 2015 she joined Hexi and was almost immediately made a major shareholder: Kang transferred her a **25% stake** (Kang kept about 48.75%, partner **Zhu Xiaoming** 26.25%) [2] [3].
- In March 2016 a further investment firm linked to the couple was set up; a month later it acquired a 48% stake, and Huang took senior management roles and began managing funds alongside Kang.
- Co-founder Zhu Xiaoming fell out with Kang after the ownership changes, left in 2017 and the dispute went to court — leaving Kang and Huang running the firm.

Kang's regulatory record:

- **2018:** Hexi investigated over stock-market manipulation and disclosure violations; firm fined just under ¥18m, Kang personally ¥400,000.
- **2025:** fined a further ¥2.5m for concealing control of a listed company.
- **2026:** subject of a criminal investigation over suspected market manipulation.
- Huang was **not** named in any of these actions, despite being a major shareholder and holding a business card as general manager/CEO.

## 3. The 2019 transaction — the missing link

- A company controlled by Huang took part in a deal worth **¥1 billion (≈US$140m)**. The buyer was Hong Kong-listed Chinese gaming company **7Road**; the seller was a British Virgin Islands company wholly owned by Huang, so the proceeds (≈$140m) sat **outside mainland China**.
- The gaming business sold had previously been connected to Hexi's investment network, passing through another Hexi-linked company associated with a woman the transcript calls "Hang", allegedly Huang's sister. Huang's Chinese name is **Huang Le** (黄乐) [4].
- **Timing anomaly:** HKEX filings show 7Road began negotiating in December 2018 — months before the offshore holding company used in the sale was set up. So who owned the business when talks began is unexplained.
- The filings confirm the ¥1bn deal but not how Huang acquired the assets, what she paid, her profit, or where the money went.
- Caixin's version: 7Road paid ¥1bn for **12 gaming businesses** through an offshore structure in which Huang was registered shareholder and Hexi the ultimate controlling party [5].
- After this, Huang **disappears from public view for about four years (2019–2023)** — no traceable business activity, which the host finds highly unusual given her previous pace.

## 4. Megaspeed: from gaming to AI chips

- 7Road had set up a Singapore subsidiary in 2015 as **7Road International** [4:1].
- **March 2023** — five months after Washington's October 2022 export controls — Huang reappears as controlling shareholder, renames it **Megaspeed International** and becomes managing director [4:2].
- New business model: buy Nvidia's advanced chips, install them in South-East Asian data centres, or (allegedly) smuggle them into China.
- **November 2023** — eight months later she steps down from formal management; the company's shares are transferred to another Singapore entity, **SwiftData** [4:3]. On paper she is out; Reuters/C4ADS could not determine who ultimately controls SwiftData, and she kept appearing publicly as chair [4:4].
- **Four days after she stepped down**, Megaspeed was listed as an **Nvidia Cloud Partner** — an expansion the host calls extraordinary.
- *The Information* (report the same day) alleged Megaspeed procured **$7bn of advanced chips — double the earlier $3bn estimate** [6].
- **Alibaba connection:** Megaspeed used an Alibaba computing-capacity agreement to demonstrate demand when approaching Nvidia — giving it credible end-customer scale rather than just a business plan.
- By 2024 she had a legitimate reason to be in the room with the world's most powerful tech executives.

## 5. The relationship with Jensen Huang

- Most reports date their first meeting to an Nvidia event in California in early 2024.
- *The Information* alleged Kang Wei had known Jensen Huang since the 2010s through gaming, so some China watchers believe the couple met him far earlier — socially, if not commercially.
- June 2024: the two Huangs socialising in Taipei. May 2025: cameras captured her kissing him on the cheek outside a Taipei restaurant.
- No one has established whether the professional relationship became romantic, or whether it affected her business success.
- The host notes Jensen Huang confirmed the affair by email to all employees over the weekend, and that he is notoriously hard to replace (very flat organisation, reportedly around 60 direct reports).

## 6. The *Liberty Times* allegation (10 November)

- Taiwan's *Liberty Times* reported that Beijing had set up a special **AI working group** spanning technology, cyber-security, commerce, intelligence, political influence and the military, with a dedicated **chip unit** inside the Ministry of State Security.
- It named **Chen Yixin**, Minister of State Security — the civilian intelligence chief, seen as the Chinese equivalent of CIA plus FBI — as having direct oversight of Huang [1:1] [7]. Chen spent much of his career in Zhejiang while Xi Jinping was the province's top official, and was appointed in October 2022, days after Xi secured a third term.
- Background: two months before the scandal, Chen published an article calling AI a major battleground in great-power rivalry, discussing advanced computing chips and the need to break through foreign technology restrictions.
- **Alleged mission one:** obtain advanced US AI chips and computing equipment via third-country companies — exactly the Megaspeed model.
- **Alleged mission two:** get close to Jensen Huang not mainly to gather information but to **influence US chip policy**, because he is one of the most influential voices arguing over whether American firms should keep selling to China. If Beijing could get an American CEO to make that case, it would not have to make it itself.
- The host stresses this is an **influence operation, not classic espionage** — the objective is the person who shapes policy, not a stolen blueprint. She explicitly does **not** accuse Jensen Huang of knowingly participating.
- Second *Liberty Times* claim: Huang was **not the only woman** given such assignments; others had already entered US tech companies at various levels, targeting export restrictions, trusted supply chains and policy networks. No individuals or companies were named.

## 7. Loose threads and open questions the host raises

- Her career path — nurse → journalist → private banking → securities → private investment → Chinese gaming → offshore finance → the centre of the AI boom — requires a different expertise, capital base and network at every step. One financier cannot explain all of it.
- The unexplained four-year gap (2019–2023) raises the question of training or recruitment.
- Was she a talented businesswoman recruited later, or cultivated from the start for a larger mission?
- **Why so high-profile?** She posted intimate photos with Jensen Huang on WeChat and was filmed publicly. Three theories, which she put to a viewer poll:
  1. Carelessness/vanity — she lost her head over the attention.
  2. Deliberate: Beijing wanted exposure, with the aim of damaging or discrediting the man leading America's AI revolution.
  3. Her visibility *was* her cover — the best place to hide is in plain sight.
- She notes theory 2 contradicts the stated mission of *influencing* Jensen Huang, so the motives pull in opposite directions.
- Audience result: an **overwhelming vote for 2**, with almost no support for 1 or 3.

## 8. Q&A highlights

- On education: she believes the graduation photo is genuine and that the absence of any stated degree is itself telling.
- On a viewer's point that chip smuggling needs no spying — Megaspeed bought legitimately; the real goal may have been lobbying to relax export controls: she engages but does not concede.
- On the "PR offensive" — Jensen Huang holding hands with his wife in public, and a staged on-stage tribute in South Korea with his wife in the audience: read as evidence a crisis was already known about, and that a scandal was about to break. *Wired* reporting that he hired a scandal PR firm last year is cited as consistent.
- On the *Financial Times*/Caixin reporting: she accepts Kang and Huang are married, notes the start date is unknown.
- On her own reactions: "I'm not jealous of Alice… I feel bad for his family… I'm not interested in any man who falls for Alice Huang." She describes it as a story about male ego.
- Brief aside on reports of a large COVID outbreak in China, with an anonymous Chinese CDC official quoted in the *Epoch Times* saying COVID remains the number-one outbreak, followed by two types of flu.
- She also floats, explicitly unattributed and unproven, that Jensen Huang's son had been "targeted by some Chinese women", and says it is worth investigating.
