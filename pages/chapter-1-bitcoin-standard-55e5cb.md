## 1. The problem money solves

Money solves an ancient and persistent problem: **how to move economic value across time and space**. By understanding the dimensions of that problem, we can understand the solution and hence the properties we want in money.

### Barter (direct exchange)

- The first solution is **barter**: two parties directly swap goods each wants — "I give you what I have, you give me what I have."
- It works only in **small circles with few people and few goods**. Example given: an island society of 20 people with about five goods (two or three staple foods, perhaps a weapon, perhaps clothing).
- In that setting goods can be priced directly against one another — e.g. **one rabbit = two fish**, **one fish = three apples**, **one knife = four fish** — and informal credit/tallying is possible ("give me rabbits over the next week or month").
- As the **number of people and goods grows, barter breaks down**: more goods, more divergent valuations, and the good I hold is often not the good you want.

### The coincidence of wants

The core problem: *how can I exchange directly with you when what I want differs from what you have to offer?* It is **exacerbated by growth in trade, specialisation and product variety** — the more people specialise and the more goods exist, the worse direct exchange performs.

---

## 2. The four dimensions of the problem

The lecture identifies the mismatch of goods themselves, plus **three further dimensions**:

| Dimension | Problem | Illustration from the lecture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The good itself** | The good I want is not the good you hold | General failure of direct exchange |
| **Scale** | Wants coincide, but the quantities/sizes do not | You want my house; I want shoes — but I don't want a house's worth of shoes |
| **Space** | Goods are in different locations and cannot be moved | You want to sell a house in your city to buy one in mine |
| **Time** | Goods are useful now but do not hold value | An apple farmer cannot pile up apples to buy a house — they rot |

The speaker flags **time as "the more interesting problem"**.

---

## 3. The solution: indirect exchange and the medium of exchange

- **Indirect exchange** overcomes all four dimensions: you sell what you have for something you do not want for its own sake, but which the other party *will* accept, and then hand that over.
- A **medium of exchange** is defined as *a good acquired purely in order to be exchanged* — not consumed, not used in production. "You acquire that good purely to sell it."
- **Anything can serve as a medium of exchange**, but some things do the job far better. Over time, superior media displace inferior ones through **network effects**, converging on a **generalised medium of exchange** — and *that* is **money**.
- Illustrative chain of inconvenience: you want my apples, I don't want your oranges (I want bananas); you must find a banana-holder, who may not want oranges either but peaches — and so on, each step adding friction.
- **Money** is therefore the generalised medium of exchange: the one *used and accepted by a large and growing number of people*, not an ad hoc medium used by one individual.

---

## 4. Money as a distinct class of economic good

Three types of economic good are distinguished:

| Type | Acquired for | Return | Liquidity/risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Consumption goods** | Their own sake (fish, bananas, a yacht) | None (direct enjoyment) | Consumed, not held |
| **Capital goods** | Use in producing other goods (fishing rod, fishing boat, banana farm) | Yes — but uncertain and future-dated | Less liquid, transaction costs, risk of breakage/failure |
| **Money (exchange good)** | Purely to exchange it | None | Most liquid, least uncertain |

**Why hold money at all?** Holding money means forgoing consumption or capital goods — a real cost. But money is the **most liquid and least risky** holding: its value does not depend on uncertain future returns. Ultimately money is held **for uncertainty**: if we knew exactly what we would want, where and when, we would hold only consumer and capital goods. Money provides **optionality**.

---

## 5. Menger and salability

- **Carl Menger**, founder of the Austrian School, identifies **salability** as money's key property: *the ease with which a good can be sold on the market whenever its holder desires, with the least loss in price.*
- Money is the solution to the coincidence of wants problem, and **the degree to which it solves that problem is the degree to which it is salable**. A widely accepted money can be taken to market without its price collapsing under the weight of the sale.

---

## 6. Deriving money's properties from the three dimensions

The speaker presents this derivation as **his own original formulation**, combining insights from Menger, Rothbard and others. The claim: the desirable properties of money **follow logically from where direct exchange fails**.

| Dimension | Required property | Reasoning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scale** | **Divisible and groupable** | Must be small enough to buy a shoe yet aggregable enough to buy a house (e.g. sell a thousand shoes, buy a house) |
| **Space** | **Portable** | Land and houses fail; metals succeed — value can be carried in a pocket, and moved while retaining integrity |
| **Time** | **Durable** | Perishables (bananas, fish) fail; the longer physical integrity is maintained, the better |
| **Time (deeper)** | **Hard (supply-resistant)** | More crucial than physical durability: the ability to **maintain value** over time, which depends on supply |

**Key argument:** divisibility, groupability, portability and durability are **common** — metallurgy and industrial production have supplied them for centuries and even millennia (iron, copper, gold, silver). **Hardness is the scarce, difficult, decisive property.**

---

## 7. Hardness and the stock-to-flow ratio

- **Hardness** = the difficulty of generating more units of the medium of exchange. It determines how good a money is as a **store of value**.
- **Hard money**: supply hard to increase significantly. **Easy money**: supply easy to increase.
- **"Hard currency"** in everyday usage (Venezuela, Turkey, Argentina, Lebanon) refers to foreign currencies harder to inflate than the local one — but the concept generalises to **all** money, not just state money.
- **Metric — stock-to-flow ratio:**
  - **Stock** = existing stockpile (all past production minus what has been consumed or destroyed).
  - **Flow** = current-year production added to the stock.
- **Low stock-to-flow = easy money.** For goods consumed as they are produced — **oil, copper, most industrial commodities** — stocks are roughly in the range of annual production, giving a ratio of about **1 (or 2)**; strategic petroleum reserves are small relative to annual consumption. A rise in production can overwhelm stocks and crash the price, so such goods do not function as money.
- **High stock-to-flow = hard money.** Flow is small relative to stock, so even a large increase in production barely moves the price; value is maintained.

### The "easy money trap"

- Anything can be used as money — but adopting something as a store of value **adds monetary demand** to its existing (e.g. industrial) demand, raising its price and therefore its production.
- If stock-to-flow is low, the resulting production **overwhelms the stockpile and crashes the price**, destroying the wealth of those who stored value in it. Copper is the lecture's example.
- **Corollary:** anything successfully used as money must have some **natural or artificial mechanism restricting new flow** to market — i.e. it must sustain a high stock-to-flow ratio.
- What ends up being used as money in any time or place is therefore **whatever has the highest stock-to-flow ratio**.
- **"There's no right or wrong medium of exchange, but there are consequences to choices."** Use easy money and you lose wealth; use hard money and you keep it — "the brutal economic reality of monetary competition."

---

## 8. Why hard money wins

- Whether through **prospective rational calculation** or the **retrospective harsh lessons of reality**, wealth concentrates in the hardest money: wealth stored in easy assets is dissipated, so only wealth in hard assets survives.
- Because hard money appreciates relative to alternatives, it accumulates the **largest cash balances**, and hence the **greatest liquidity** and widest acceptance — i.e. the greatest **salability** in Menger's sense. Soft monies "continuously bleed value."
- **Money is a network good**: its value depends on the size of the network using it. Larger networks attract more demand, producing a **winner-take-all dynamic**. The second-best money offers nowhere near the advantage of the dominant one, so markets trend towards **one money, or very few**.
- Technology constantly changes the relative hardness of candidate monies, and the free market continuously selects among them.

---

## 9. The three functions of money and their consequences

1. **Medium of exchange** — the essential function.
2. **Store of value** — necessary for it to serve as a medium of exchange.
3. **Unit of account** — all prices expressed in one good.

**Why the unit of account matters:** under direct exchange, every good's price must be expressed in terms of every other good, so each added good multiplies the number of prices. With one money, each new good adds **only one** price. This efficiency is a further driver of winner-take-all convergence.

**A universal money enables:**

- **Economic calculation** — accurate production and consumption decisions, raising efficiency and productivity.
- **Growing division of labour** — producers produce for the market in general rather than for specific counterparties, enormously raising productivity and wealth.
- **Complex capital production** — saving and accumulation permit longer, more roundabout and more productive processes; the lecture frames **civilisation itself** as the continual adoption of more complex methods of capital production.

These three themes are flagged for detailed treatment in **chapters 5, 6 and 7**.

---

## 10. Conclusion of the lecture

- Many goods have served as money historically, but money is **predominantly the hardest-to-produce liquid commodity that is also divisible, groupable and transportable**. Many goods are divisible, groupable and transportable; **only one can be hardest to produce**.
- As technology advances, that commodity can change; **monetary transitions can almost always be understood in terms of changes in hardness**, which drive a money's rise or demise.
