# Criminal Lagarde

![Criminal Lagarde](https://storage.ghost.io/c/9c/2a/9c2aa86d-156e-419f-9a0f-38bf91aebf15/content/images/size/w2000/2026/11/The-Real-Reason-Lagarde-Is-LEAVING-The-ECB.jpg)

Christine Lagarde announced at the end of October that she will step down as ECB President on **7 February 2027**, roughly **nine months before her term expires** on 31 October 2027 [1] [2]. Her exit coincides with two other senior vacancies, so three of the most powerful jobs in European finance are being filled in one round — through a process the video argues is being stitched up before French voters get a say.

## The official reason

At her press conference Lagarde said she wants:

> “the freedom of speech, the freedom of expression, and the freedom to engage on matters that are not monetary policy matters.”

The video’s rebuttal: she runs the institution that sets the price of money for the entire eurozone — shaping mortgage costs in Madrid and corporate borrowing costs in Munich — and after seven years her complaint is that the role muzzles her. (Reuters reported her saying “I don’t think that I can remain neutral” [3].)

## Theory one: she is leaving an economy she couldn’t fix

- **German industry** pays more for energy than US or Chinese competitors, and part of that is self-inflicted: the post-2022 scramble to rebuild gas supply, plus EU carbon pricing under the **ETS**, added a homemade surcharge.
- Result: chemicals, metals and carmakers are **restructuring, cutting back and relocating**.
- On the day of her announcement the ECB **held the deposit rate at 2.5%** (confirmed: 2.50%, after a 25bp rise in September) [4] [5], flagging **upside inflation risks from energy** alongside **downside growth risks** — the combination central bankers dread.
- The **2024 Draghi report** on competitiveness diagnosed the problem; implementation has been “sluggish, to put it politely”, slowed by political fragmentation.
- Lagarde’s consistent line: **structural reform is for national governments**, not the ECB. The video’s charge: Brussels writes the rules and Frankfurt sets the money, but neither accepts responsibility for the outcome.

## Theory two: France — the problem the ECB is dreading

- Public debt around **120% of GDP**; deficit above **5%** a year.
- In late October the **10-year borrowing cost hit 5.14%**, described as a 24-year high, with the **spread over Germany at ~175bp** — the widest since 2011 (see caveat below).
- Politics: Macron has no majority; the minority government under **Sébastien Lecornu** is deadlocked on the budget; **Fitch** and **Moody’s** both have France on negative outlook; the economy **shrank 0.1%** in Q3 while the wider eurozone grew.
- The ECB’s tool for this is the **Transmission Protection Instrument (TPI)**, which can buy a country’s bonds to cap its borrowing costs — but only for states complying with EU fiscal rules, avoiding severe imbalances, with sustainable debt and sound policies.
- France is under an **excessive deficit procedure**, so it **fails the entry tests for its own safety net**. Northern creditor members would resist bending the rules for France, since that would reward the country that cannot pass a budget.
- **The Lagarde angle:** she is French, served as finance minister under Sarkozy, and in December 2016 a French court found her **negligent** over a **€404 million** state payout to Bernard Tapie — no penalty, no criminal record. A French ECB president signing off on a French rescue would be toxic in Berlin and The Hague; refusing one would be unforgivable in Paris. Leaving early means she is **not holding the pen** when the decision lands.

## Theory three: the succession is being carved up before French voters decide

France elects a president in **April and May 2027**. Had Lagarde served to October, her successor would have been chosen with a new French president — possibly from the **National Rally (Le Pen)** — at the table. Leaving in February hands the pick to **Macron, Friedrich Merz** and other EU leaders while Macron still holds power.

| Post | Departure | Reported allocation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Presidency | Lagarde, 7 Feb 2027 | Spain or the Netherlands |
| German board seat | **Isabel Schnabel**, 3 Jan 2027 (to the IMF) | Germany keeps the seat |
| Chief economist | **Philip Lane**, 31 May 2027 | France |

Named candidates in the video: **Pablo Hernández de Cos** (former Bank of Spain governor), **Klaas Knot** (former Dutch central bank chief), **Nadia Calviño** (EIB president), plus a Bundesbank name (garbled in the transcript — see caveats). Reporting confirms the race has narrowed to **Knot and de Cos** as the only two nominees [6] [7].

Fallout in France: the **National Rally** accuses Macron of locking down institutions before losing power, and **Mélenchon’s** camp is equally angry — two movements that agree on nothing agreeing on this.

## Her record, as the video frames it

- **12 March 2020:** with markets in pandemic freefall, Lagarde said the ECB was “not here to close spreads” — triggering a historic Italian bond sell-off and a full-blown panic, after which the ECB launched **PEPP** to buy bonds.
- **“Transitory” inflation:** the ECB insisted the 2021–22 surge would pass; it didn’t.
- **July 2022 – September 2023:** rates raised by **4.5 percentage points**, deposit rate from **−0.5% to 4%** — the fastest hiking cycle on record. Late to spot inflation, then hard on the brakes, with households and businesses absorbing the pain.
- **Digital euro:** in preparation phase; Parliament and Council are in **trilogue** negotiations; pilot eyed for **2027**, possible first issuance around **2029** if the law passes. The video’s verdict: economies drowning in debt, industry struggling, leaders “busy figuring out how to reinvent the euro as an app”.

## The closing argument

The ECB’s identity rests on independence from politics, yet its top jobs are being shared out “like cabinet posts” with national quotas and a deadline set by incumbents racing the French vote. Lagarde is **rumoured** to be in line to lead the **World Economic Forum (WEF)** — consistent with her stated desire for a platform. She told *La Croix* that running for French president was “not a good idea”: freedom of speech, yes; facing voters, no. The through-line: the officials who made the errors never pay a price, while the German worker whose plant is restructured, the family paying more at the till and the French taxpayer covering higher interest do.
