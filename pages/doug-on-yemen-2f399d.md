## Geopolitics: Yemen, the Houthis and Saudi Arabia

- The Houthis are a rebel group, not Yemen's official government, yet they control roughly 75% of the country and people, including the capital — so "calling them the Houthis is a fiction."
- They are "sandal-clad, really tough mountain warriors" whose success (shutting the East–West pipeline, downing an F-15) is shocking, even if the aircraft was Saudi-operated.
- Casey speculates that if he were running Yemen, overthrowing the Saudi regime would be a natural move: MBS has made "criminally stupid mistakes" (the 100-kilometre-long desert city "going back to the desert"), Saudi society lives off stolen oil money, the Shi'a are concentrated where the oil is, and the Americans have passed on helping MBS. "I think that could be something that happens nobody's talking about."
- It is "ridiculous that a country is named after the family that took it over about 100 years ago."
- The closure of the Bab-el-Mandeb strait and the East–West pipeline has cut off oil to Europe (Saudi Arabia reportedly cancelling next month's European exports) — a "big one on the horizon" that could drag in other players.
- With record diesel and jet fuel prices, "something's got to give pretty soon."

## Markets and the Economy

- Casey's positioning: long **gold, gold stocks, agricultural products, and energy (oil, gas, uranium)** — "that's all we can do about it as little people."
- On proposals to ban US diesel exports: Trump "flies by the seat of his pants," thinking only of the immediate and direct consequences, never the indirect and delayed ones — "thinking like an economist" means the latter, and "a reasonably intelligent six-year-old can predict immediate and direct consequences." Export bans would invite tit-for-tat retaliation (he cites Canada's ~$20bn response and its EU associate-membership talks).
- He characterises Trump as a "megalomaniac who sometimes seems like he's bordering on the ragged edge of criminal psychopathy."
- The Fed raised rates despite Trump's threats; Republicans are expected to lose both House and Senate in November, after which "the Democrats will undoubtedly go on attack."
- With ~$2 trillion fiscal deficits, the government "has to keep printing money," but society carries far more debt than in the early 1980s (when short-term rates hit 15–18%), so rates cannot be raised that high — yet rates will rise as the dollar declines. Verdict: "I remain unremittingly gloomy and bearish."
- We are "clearly at the peak of a stock market super ultra bubble"; when it bursts, pension plans and savings will shrink "in their present form or size" — the next financial crisis "is on the way."

## AI: Fear, Regulation and the Chinese Threat

- The current doom talk (the ">10% chance of human extinction" narrative) is, in Casey's co-host's analysis, largely a way to induce fear so that frontier labs (OpenAI, Claude) can capture the regulatory regime and keep out competition.
- Chinese models are roughly "six months behind and 1/40th of the cost" — adequate for most uses. Example: a major law firm (referred to as "Laam," likely Latham & Watkins) is building in-house AI on a Chinese base model rather than handing proprietary data to US frontier labs, cutting them out of the enterprise market where the big money is.
- Casey sees no return on the hundreds of billions (much of it borrowed) going into AI data centres; failures could trigger massive bankruptcies — and, since the "race with China must be won," a government takeover of "too big to fail" AI companies.
- Cites the Marx/ Hegel line: the first time is tragedy, the second time is farce (low comedy).

## Taking Money from the State

- Casey is "very sympathetic" to refusing state payments — accepting them makes you "a party to theft by receiving stolen goods."
- But he notes the conundrum: Ayn Rand, iron-bound in her morality, collected Social Security as a rebate on taxes she had paid.
- His ultimate solution: abolish the state itself, which he believes serves no useful purpose. On a personal level, it is "a personal thing" like abortion.
- The co-host adds that accepting state money forces you to see yourself as a victim; Casey: "we're trapped in the matrix."

## Investment Method

- **No stop-losses in illiquid resource stocks:** specialists watching the book will "pick you off" — a stop can end up being the low tick of the day. Instead, use discipline: if a position falls by more than a third, admit you're wrong and dump it — you can always buy back later. Dump it even if it is falling "for reasons you can't figure out."
- **Private deals:** he's had some good luck but dislikes them — no control, no visibility, no guaranteed exit, no updates. Jim Rogers refuses them entirely simply to keep his life uncluttered — "he's a very smart guy... I think you got to listen to what Jimmy says." Pre-IPO stakes can be great but are a gamble you probably don't need.

## Drones and Nanotechnology

- The counter to military drones is "smaller, cheaper, faster drones" — swarms of thousands of cheap "bumblebee" drones rather than the US model of a $30m MQ-9 and a ~$250m high-altitude drone, each a "costly disaster" when lost. China is on the leading edge of swarming.
- Casey calls himself "the original nanotech bull," invoking Eric Drexler's vision: submicroscopic assemblers controlled by submicroscopic supercomputers, rearranging matter at the atomic level — "take pixie dust... toss it on a desk and it will disassemble the desk into its component elements and reassemble them into anything you want... total and absolute magic."
- The same tech could turn a person into "pink goo"; grey-goo planetary scenarios are dismissed for now — too energy-intensive. It "could be fairly soon. These things move quickly sometimes."
