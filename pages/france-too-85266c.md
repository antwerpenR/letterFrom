## The visible crisis: fuel shortages

- Roughly **one in six petrol stations** in France has run out of some type of fuel (over one in four in some areas), with drivers relying on government price sites and crowdsourced shortage maps.
- The trigger: **TotalEnergies capped prices at €1.99/litre for petrol and €2.25 for diesel** across 3,300 stations; everywhere else prices kept rising, with diesel hitting a record national average of €2.41 and motorway stations near €2.80. Panic buying followed, and ~90% of the stations in shortage were Total's.
- On paper France has ~117 days of strategic reserves (above the EU's 90-day minimum), yet there is no rationing — just police tape across empty pumps.
- The state responded with **meetings, not action**: an 18 September crisis room gathering at the Élysée ("Vega"), then a **€450 million package** (expanded frequent-driver payment for ~5.5 million people, fishermen's rebate at the EU maximum, discounts for farmers and builders, emergency waivers).
- The accounting makes it worse: fuel consumption fell ~30% in 10 days, costing **€47 million in lost fuel tax**, on top of the €450 million payout — temporary workarounds rather than fixes.

## The root cause: a broken Parliament (2024)

- In June 2024, Macron's centrists took ~15% in the European elections against 31.5% for Le Pen's National Rally; that night Macron **dissolved the National Assembly** — the first such dissolution since 1997.
- The snap election three weeks later produced a hung chamber: New Popular Front 180 seats, Macron's bloc 159, National Rally and allies 142 — **289 needed for a majority**.
- Any two blocs can combine to bring a government down via a **censure motion**, but no two will govern together: "a parliament that nobody can govern and everybody can veto."

## Four governments since September 2024

| Prime Minister | Outcome |
| --- | --- |
| **Michel Barnier** (Sept 2024) | Pushed budget through without a vote; censured in December 2024 with 331 votes (288 needed) — first government toppled by no-confidence since 1962. |
| **François Bayrou** | Staked everything on a €44 billion austerity plan, called a confidence vote on himself, and lost it 364–194 in September 2025 — the first government to fall that way under the Fifth Republic. |
| **Sébastien Lecornu** (first attempt) | Spent a month forming a cabinet, then **resigned roughly 14 hours after naming it** when coalition partners revolted — the shortest-lived government in Fifth Republic history. |
| **Lecornu** (reappointed 4 days later) | Survived to date, but at a price: he **suspended the 2023 pension reform** (retirement age 62 → 64) until after the 2027 presidential election, trading away Macron's signature second-term reform. He has since survived multiple censure attempts — over the EU's Mercosur deal, the budget (one within 19 votes of passing), and an energy decree. |

## The deficit and the bond market

- France runs the **largest budget deficit in the Eurozone: 5.1% of GDP for 2025** against the EU's 3% rule.
- Ten-year borrowing costs are at their highest since 2008; the spread over Germany has blown past 1 percentage point for the first time since the Eurozone debt crisis; **France now pays more than Italy to borrow**.
- The 2026 budget never won a vote — it passed via **Article 49.3** (used by Lecornu in January for revenue, again for spending, adopted in February), the very tool that got Barnier censured 14 months earlier; the only difference was that the opposition couldn't agree on how to kill it.
- For 2027, Lecornu has put **€54 billion of cuts** on the table — €10 billion more than Bayrou's sacked austerity plan — with left and right already opposed.

## Credit rating downgrades (2025)

- **Fitch** cut France from AA− to A+ in September 2025 (four days after Bayrou fell) — first loss of double-A in over a decade; **S&P** followed in October; **Moody's** kept its rating but shifted the outlook to negative, blaming political fragmentation blocking deficit cuts.

## The street decides when Parliament can't

- Recurring unrest: Yellow Vests over fuel tax (2018, occupying roundabouts nationwide); months of strikes over pension reform in 2023, which Élisabeth Borne forced through using Article 49.3 **23 times**; farmer tractor convoys (2024); a "block everything" day of strikes in September 2025, two days after Bayrou's collapse.
- The pension fight shows protest works — with a two-year delay: the street lost in 2023, then won in 2025 when Lecornu suspended the reform to survive.
- Most serious recent case: riots and looting across the country after the fatal police shooting of 17-year-old **Nahel Merzouk in Nanterre** (summer 2023), with elite police deployed and neighbourhoods effectively beyond the state's reach.
- Current flare-ups: fishermen blockading the Fos-sur-Mer fuel depot near Marseille (France's largest, normally dispatching 900+ trucks a day) on 15 September, leaving 100–150 tankers stuck; former Yellow Vests marching on a prefecture on the 19th; and a national call for roundabout occupations in October that originated not from unions or figureheads but from **AI-generated posters on TikTok shared tens of thousands of times** — "a national protest organised by nobody in particular."

## The courts as the final authority

- **Nicolas Sarkozy**, convicted of criminal conspiracy over illegal Libyan financing of his 2007 campaign, entered **La Santé prison in Paris** in September 2025 — the first former Fifth Republic president imprisoned — before being released under judicial supervision 20 days later pending appeal.
- **Marine Le Pen**, convicted of embezzling ~€3 million of European Parliament assistants' funds, saw the Paris Court of Appeal uphold the conviction but swap her prison term for electronic monitoring and cut her ban from office — just enough to let her stand. Her appeal to the Cour de cassation suspends the sentence; the court could rule by **April 2027, with the presidential first round on 18 April** — potentially weeks before the vote.

## Conclusion

The video's verdict: France "no longer functions" — it "produces nothing but meetings, subsidies and censure motions", leaving the street and the courts to settle what happens next. Since September 2024 France has had four governments, lost its double-A rating, and seen a former president imprisoned. The host closes by asking viewers whether France has a broken political system, or a people who simply won't let anyone govern them.
