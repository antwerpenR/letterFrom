# Frank on street photography

![Frank on street photography](https://storage.ghost.io/c/9c/2a/9c2aa86d-156e-419f-9a0f-38bf91aebf15/content/images/size/w2000/2026/11/10-Street-Photography-Tips-You-Need-to-Know.jpg)

Frank opens by admitting his first attempts at street photography were unremarkable, and that the improvement came from a set of repeatable techniques rather than new gear. He stresses that the ten tips overlap and blend in practice — "a bit of that one mixed with a bit of that one" — and that the aim is for them to become instinctive [1]. ### The ten tips at a glance

| # | Tip | Core technique |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Walk with your camera | Screen flipped out, camera at waist level, focus limiter set to 2 m–infinity so you never have to focus manually |
| 2 | Wait in one location | Pick a spot with heavy footfall or activity, then wait for something interesting to enter the frame |
| 3 | Find an interesting background | Match a striking backdrop (mural, poster, shop front) with a subject that completes or contrasts with it |
| 4 | Use the light | Strong sun, hard shadows and silhouettes; shoot at the right time of day and in the right weather |
| 5 | Reflections | Mirrors, glass, large windows and water; wait for the subject to arrive |
| 6 | Isolation | A single figure alone in the frame — easiest when crowds are thin |
| 7 | Activities | Demonstrations, street performers, people at work, public events |
| 8 | Look up | Architecture, converging buildings, features overhead, aircraft |
| 9 | Create a collection | Set yourself one subject for the day (doorways, puddles, shop fronts) and shoot only that |
| 10 | Framing | Use columns, doorways, alley mouths and hatches to frame the subject |

### The tips in detail

**1. Walk with your camera.** The screen is flipped out so he can shoot from the waist without raising the camera to his eye. A focus limiter (found in the camera menu) set from **2 m to infinity** removes the need to focus at all, whether by back-button or otherwise. He demonstrates this while walking, catching a man in an Afghan coat, a man sitting on steps beside his mobility scooter, and a woman drinking coffee — then continues walking without breaking stride. He insists the hit rate is surprisingly good, but only if you stay "super observant."

**2. Wait in one location.** As he has grown older he increasingly prefers sitting still to walking. The requirement is an interesting location with plenty of activity or foot traffic. Examples:

- A wall in front of a famous library building, where children jumped up and walked towards him.
- A park bench by the sea, catching a man with a backpack against the Liverpool skyline.
- Outside a local cinema whose window displayed a large image of people in 3D glasses — he waited for an interesting passer-by to complete the shot.

**3. Find an interesting background.** Closely related to waiting, but the background leads. Examples include a street-art mural titled *One Man*, completed by one man walking into shot; a large poster at a bus stop with two men standing either side; a giant dog mural paired with a dog walker; a charity-shop worker outside on a cigarette break; and an old building sign reading "strippers" — in fact a paint-stripping firm — with a man nonchalantly walking a dog past.

**4. Use the light.** Hard, high-contrast sunlight produces shadows, hard edges and silhouettes, and he notes these print well for wall display. Time of day changes the shadows, so it must be learned. Examples: a huge doorway casting shadow on the Liverpool riverfront; a backlit skater; a shaft of light under an escalator catching two people beneath the stairs; a man eating an ice cream with a seagull sweeping in behind him; leading lines up to an angular museum; a "tale of two halves" with one figure emerging from shadow and another in harsh light; early-morning shadows from a handrail converging with steps; long shadows of a lone walker; and two men casting two identical shadows — "everything in double."

**5. Reflections.** He calls these easy: find a reflective surface — glass, mirrors or water — and wait. Techniques shown:

- Shooting into shop mirrors so the subject is framed inside the glass, unaware.
- Waiting for a figure to step onto a bridge above water.
- Learning where the reliable reflections are: large panes of glass, big windows.
- A fisheye shot of the Liver Building reflected in the glass opposite — he later sold the lens because he was nicknamed "Frank the fisheye photographer."
- A café shot reflecting the Albert Dock and a carousel, which he values as a piece of social history.
- A mask-wearing woman reflected in a gallery window during the tail end of the COVID epidemic.
- A Madrid "lake" that was in fact a wet pavement, roughly half an inch to an inch deep.

*Caveat:* puddle reflections need calm conditions — wind or chop destroys them.

**6. Isolation.** Find a subject entirely alone. Examples: a figure climbing steps by the seaside; an elderly woman isolated against a Madrid shop front, where the juxtaposition does the work; a woman reading on her own at the top of a leading line; a lone man in Liverpool's main shopping complex, only possible because the epidemic had emptied it; and a sulking boy who had walked away from his family — a shot that reminded Frank of his own childhood, when his mother insisted he wore a hat. He also singles out the flight of birds in one isolation frame.

**7. Activities.** Any street activity creates a "big jumble of interesting things." He adds a caution: be careful what you point your camera at, provided the demonstration is peaceful. Examples: Morris dancers in Mathew Street, including a young man in a dress and wig with hairy legs, apparently fundraising; a limbo performer under a flame, where the crowd's reactions make the picture; an LGBTQ demonstration; three women at Pride with flags draped round them, each looking a different way; a queue of Goths outside a shop in York (shot from waist height with the focus limiter, precisely because stopping to raise the camera might have provoked complaint); a woman being taught to skate by two helpers; and a man delivering potatoes — "a piece of social history."

**8. Look up.** We rarely do it. Mostly this yields architecture, but the results can be striking: three buildings converging against sky with clouds reflected in the windows; protruding glass panes that look like science fiction; a sharp high-contrast design feature; a doughnut truck angled so the Liver Building peeks over its roof; and a jet plane crossing above a colourful facade. One shot came from testing a zoom lens at a funfair ride on the riverfront.

**9. Create a collection.** Decide before leaving the house that you will photograph one thing only — doorways, puddles, reflections, market traders — which narrows your focus and forces you to work a theme. His Madrid set covered shop, bar and restaurant fronts with people included: two friends sharing stories outside a café; a repair shop with a woman hemming jeans; a barber's, lit by strong light through the window and layered with a reflection; an old man reading a newspaper; and mannequins' legs beside two older women chatting.

**10. Framing.** Use existing structure to enclose the subject: two cast-iron Victorian columns on the seafront; an alleyway between two shops; a serving hatch in his local gallery; the mouth of an alley with walls framing the figure at its end — where, with deliberate irony, a blind man walked through "a blind alley." He calls that serendipity: "if you wait, if you plan, these things can happen." A final example at Anfield Stadium uses tiled vanishing-point lines leading up to a figure at the top of the stairs.
