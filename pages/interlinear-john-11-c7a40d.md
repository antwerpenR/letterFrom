## Full LSJ entries for the three semantically richest words

### λόγος (logos) — Strong's 3056

The LSJ entry for *λόγος* is one of the longest in the lexicon (Duke cites "five pages"). The principal senses, drawn from the LSJ tradition as reflected in Strong's and Thayer's digests of the classical entry, are:

| Section | Senses |
| --- | --- |
| **I.** | **computation, calculation, reckoning, account** (especially of money) |
| **II.** | **account, i.e. regard, consideration, value, esteem, penalty, charge** |
| **III.** | **narrative, relation, account, tale, story, history** |
| **IV.** | **speech, discourse, that which is spoken, what someone has said; a saying, utterance, declaration** |
| **V.** | **the word by which the inward thought is expressed; speech, talk, conversation** |
| **VI.** | **the thing spoken of, the subject of speech, the matter under discussion** |
| **VII.** | **reason, the faculty of reasoning; rational nature, rational faculty** |
| **VIII.** | **reason as a principle; the rational principle governing the cosmos; the universal principle of order and meaning (philosophical, esp. Stoic)** |
| **IX.** | **relation, ratio, proportion** (mathematical) |
| **X.** | **a rule, principle, law** |
| **XI.** | **a thesis, argument, proposition** (in dialectic) |
| **XII.** | **a continuing narrative or account** |

> **Key observation for this verse:** the single-word rendering "the Word" collapses all of the above into one nominalised gloss. The Greek *λόγος* is derived from **λέγω** (legō, *to say, to speak, to lay forth, to recount, to gather*), and its root sense is **an active process of articulating or conveying meaning** — computation, narration, reasoning, discourse — not a static "word." This is precisely the point Duke makes about the *logos* nominalisation.

### θεός / θεόν (theos) — Strong's 2316

| Form | Case | Senses (LSJ) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **θεόν** (v. 12, accusative) | accusative, masculine, singular — the *object* of πρὸς | **God, a god, the deity, the divine; used both in a general sense ("a god") and in a particular, singular sense ("God")** [7] |
| **θεὸς** (v. 14, nominative, **without** the article) | nominative, masculine, singular — the *predicate* | **a god, deity, divine being, the divine, God** — the anarthrous (article-less) form is grammatically significant: it functions as a *qualitative* or *categorical* predicate, describing *what the logos was* rather than *identifying* the logos as the same person as *the God* (τὸν θεόν) in the preceding clause |

> **Grammatical note (non-doctrinal):** Verse 12 has **τὸν θεόν** (accusative, *with* the article) — "the God," i.e. God as a specific, identified referent. Verse 14 has **θεὸς** (nominative, *without* the article) — functioning as a predicate nominative describing the *quality or category* of the logos. The difference between *articular* and *anarthrous* θεός here is a matter of Greek syntax, not of hermeneutic translation: the articular form points to a specific identity; the anarthrous form ascribes a quality or nature. This is the structural artefact that makes John 1:1 theologically and linguistically significant.

### πρὸς (pros) — Strong's 4314

Here πρὸς takes the **accusative** (τὸν θεόν). The LSJ senses for πρὸς + accusative are:

| Section | Senses (πρὸς + accusative) |
| --- | --- |
| **C.I.** (place, with verbs of motion) | **towards, to** |
| **C.I.** (with verbs implying previous motion) | **upon, against** |
| **C.I.** (with verbs of seeing/looking) | **towards** |
| **C.I.** (hostile sense) | **against** |
| **C.I.** (in argument/reply) | **in reply to, in argument with** |
| **C.I.** (non-hostile) | **to, towards, with reference to** |
| **C.II.** (time) | **towards or near a certain time, at or about** |
| **C.III.** (relation) | **in reference to, in respect of, touching** |
| **D.** (adverbial) | **besides, over and above** |
| **E.** (in composition) | **motion towards, addition, besides, a being on, at, by, or beside; hence a remaining beside, and metaphorically connexion and engagement with anything** |

> **Key observation:** The standard English rendering "with" (as in "the Word was *with* God") is a **hermeneutic compression** of the Greek. The LSJ's core sense for πρὸς + accusative is **direction toward, motion toward, engagement with, face-to-face orientation toward**. The adverbial/compositional sense E — *"a remaining beside, and metaphorically connexion and engagement with anything"* — comes closest to the traditional "with," but the Greek carries a stronger sense of **dynamic, directed relational engagement** than the English preposition "with" conveys. This is the kind of structural artefact Duke's method is designed to recover.

## Aramaic roots — where applicable

The Gospel of John was composed in Greek, not Aramaic, so the words in this verse are **Greek**, not Aramaic. However, there are two relevant Aramaic connections:

1. **memrā (מֵימְרָא)** — In **Aramaic Targums** (the Aramaic paraphrases of the Hebrew Scriptures used in synagogues), the divine word is rendered as **memrā** (literally "the word" or "the utterance"), functioning as a **hypostasis** — a distinct manifestation or agency of God through which God creates, reveals, and acts. Many scholars see *memrā* as a conceptual bridge between the Hebrew *dabar* (word) and the Greek *logos* of John 1:1. If there is an Aramaic conceptual substrate behind John's choice of *logos*, **memrā** is the most likely candidate — though it is an **Aramaic loan-concept**, not the etymological root of the Greek word *λόγος* itself.
2. **λόγος (logos)** — etymologically, this is a **native Greek word**, derived from the Indo-European root **\*leg-** (*to collect, gather, say, speak* — cognate with Latin *legere*, *to read/gather*). It does **not** trace to an Aramaic root. Its semantic range (computation, account, reason, discourse, proportion, principle) is distinctively Greek, not Aramaic.

**Conclusion on Aramaic tracing:** No word in John 1:1 has a direct Aramaic *etymological* root. The **conceptual** bridge is through *memrā* in the Targumic tradition, which may have influenced the theological content John assigns to *logos* — but the Greek vocabulary itself is indigenous to Greek.

## Summary of what this interlinear reveals (per Duke's methodology)

| Structural artefact | What the Greek actually shows | What "hermeneutic" translation conceals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ἐν ἀρχῇ** | "in/at [the] beginning/origin/first-principle/source" | Compressed to "in the beginning" — drops *origin*, *first principle*, *source*, *rulership* |
| **ἦν** (imfective) | "was [continuously] existing/being" — imperfect = ongoing, continuous existence | Rendered as simple past "was" — loses the durative aspect |
| **ὁ λόγος** | *the* articulating-reasoning-reckoning-narrating-discourse-principle | Frozen into the static noun "the Word" — loses computation, reason, discourse, ratio, principle |
| **πρὸς τὸν θεόν** | "toward / in directed engagement with / face-to-face with [the] God" | Rendered as "with God" — loses the directional, dynamic, relational-engagement sense |
| **θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος** (anarthrous θεός as predicate) | "[as to its nature/category] deity/divine/God was the logos" — qualitative predicate | Often rendered "the Word was God" (with articular English "God") — collapses the qualitative/categorical nuance into simple identity, and reverses the Greek word order |

This is precisely the kind of analysis Duke's prompt is designed to surface: by refusing a single hermeneutic gloss and requiring **all senses** of each word, the interlinear exposes how much semantic range the standard translation compresses — and where structural artefacts of political/doctrinal translation decisions may be visible.
