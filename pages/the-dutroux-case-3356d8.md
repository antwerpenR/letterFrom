### A National Horror

The Dutroux case centers on the crimes of **Marc Dutroux**, a convicted child molester and kidnapper, his wife **Michèle Martin**, and his accomplice **Michel Lelièvre**. Between 1995 and 1996, the group was responsible for the abduction, torture, and sexual assault of six girls, four of whom died.

**The Victims and Their Fates:**

- **Julie Lejeune (8) and Mélissa Russo (8):** Abducted in June 1995. They were held captive in a hidden, custom-built dungeon in the cellar of Dutroux's house. They tragically starved to death while Dutroux was serving a short prison sentence for an unrelated car theft. Their bodies were found buried in his garden in 1996.
- **An Marchal (17) and Eefje Lambrecks (19):** Abducted in August 1995. They were drugged, abused, and eventually murdered. Their bodies were found buried under a shed belonging to one of Dutroux's accomplices.
- **Sabine Dardenne (12):** Abducted in May 1996. She was held captive in the same hidden dungeon for 80 days.
- **Laetitia Delhez (14):** Abducted in August 1996. Her abduction led directly to Dutroux's arrest just days later.

On August 15, 1996, police finally rescued **Sabine Dardenne and Laetitia Delhez** alive from the dungeon, an event that both horrified and electrified the nation.

**Systemic Failure and Public Outrage:**

As the details of the case emerged, it became shockingly clear that the crimes could have been prevented. The investigation was plagued by:

- **Gross Incompetence:** Police had searched Dutroux's house previously but failed to find the hidden dungeon. Key leads were ignored.
- **Rivalry and Miscommunication:** Different police forces (the local police and the national Gendarmerie) failed to share crucial information with each other.
- **Corruption Allegations:** Rumors and evidence suggested that Dutroux might be part of a wider pedophile network protected by powerful figures, though this was never definitively proven in court.

The public's simmering anger boiled over in October 1996 with an event known as the **"Spaghetti Arrest."** The lead investigating judge, Jean-Marc Connerotte, who had earned the public's trust, was removed from the case on a legal technicality—he had attended a charity spaghetti dinner with the victims' families. To the public, this looked like the establishment sabotaging the one person they believed was getting close to the truth.

### The White Marches (La Marche Blanche / De Witte Mars)

The removal of Judge Connerotte was the final straw. It confirmed the public's worst fears: that the justice system was corrupt, incompetent, and protecting its own.

**The Event:**

On **October 20, 1996**, an estimated **300,000 people** descended on Brussels for a silent protest. This was the "White March." Participants wore white clothing and carried white flowers or balloons.

- **Symbolism of White:** The color white was chosen to symbolize innocence, mourning for the murdered children, hope for the survivors, and a demand for a "clean" and transparent justice system.
- **A Silent Uprising:** The march was overwhelmingly peaceful and silent, a stark contrast to the rage people felt. It was a demonstration of profound grief and a powerful, collective vote of no confidence in the Belgian state.
- **The Message:** The marchers were not just mourning the victims; they were demanding fundamental reform of Belgium's police and judicial systems. They were protesting against what they saw as a rotten system of political patronage and incompetence, often referred to as *"le pot belge"* (the Belgian mess).

**Legacy and Aftermath**

The Dutroux case and the White Marches had a profound and lasting impact on Belgium.

1. **Judicial and Police Reform:** The public pressure was so immense that it forced sweeping changes. The **"Octopus Agreement"** led to the merging of Belgium's rival police forces into one integrated structure, creating the modern Federal and Local Police. The justice system was also reformed to improve victim support.
2. **Child Focus:** A national center for missing and sexually exploited children, **Child Focus**, was created in the wake of the case and remains a vital organization today.
3. **National Trauma:** The case inflicted a deep wound on the Belgian psyche. It shattered the country's sense of security and led to a lasting distrust of political and judicial institutions.
4. **The Trial:** Marc Dutroux was finally tried in 2004 and sentenced to life in prison. Michèle Martin received a 30-year sentence and was controversially granted an early, conditional release in 2012, which sparked further public protest.

In summary, the Dutroux case was not just about the horrific crimes of one man. It exposed deep flaws within the Belgian state, triggering the White March—one of the largest public demonstrations in European history—which in turn forced the government to enact fundamental reforms that reshaped the country's institutions.
