# Who is running it?

![Who is running it?](https://storage.ghost.io/c/9c/2a/9c2aa86d-156e-419f-9a0f-38bf91aebf15/content/images/size/w2000/2026/11/Who-Is-Really-Running-the-World_--It---s-Not-Who-You-Think-.jpg)

**Thesis in one line:** Elections change the cast, not the machinery; what actually runs modern societies is a layered "operating system" of institutional continuity, a moral-free monetary worldview, unexamined shared assumptions, generational timescales, and — at the base — shared belief.

The monologue is structured as five descending layers, then a unifying answer. Citations below are to the transcript (supplied as a single continuous line, so I cite by section and quote rather than by timestamp). Quotations are verbatim apart from restored punctuation.

### 1. The opening challenge: democracy as a partial story

- The viewer is told that "your vote shapes the future… that the world is run by the people for the people."
- The counter-image is biological: **no cell in your body voted for your breakfast**, yet each "just does its job inside a system far larger than it can see."
- The framing question: what if society works the same way — and "the forces shaping your life don't think in election cycles, but in generations"?
- The stated aim is not to debunk democracy but to expose "the operating system underneath the story."

### 2. Layer one: elections change faces, not machinery

- A fair concession first: "Voting matters. Elections remove governments peacefully, which most of human history never managed."
- What elections change: "the faces, the tone, the slogans on the podium."
- What they rarely change: "the central bank's mandate, the treaties, the trade rules, the regulators, the permanent civil service, the financial system."
- **The revolving door:** a class of people "move between these worlds with ease — government one decade, a bank or consultancy the next, then an international institution, a foundation, a board seat."
- Supporting example given: in America, **three Treasury Secretaries since the mid-1990s came from the same investment bank**, appointed by both parties.
- The key mechanism is *convergence, not conspiracy*: "None of this requires a secret plan… They don't need to coordinate. They already agree."
- Conclusion: "the claim isn't that your vote is worthless. It's that the range of possible outcomes is narrower than it looks" — and "most people never notice the walls because they've never tried to walk through one."

### 3. Layer two: money as a religion without morals

The argument that the monetary system has inherited religion's old social function, and satisfies every structural requirement of a religion:

| Religious element | The monetary equivalent (as stated) |
| --- | --- |
| High priests | Central bankers, whose "every word is studied like scripture"; markets "can move billions on a single adjective" |
| Sacred texts | GDP figures, balance sheets, credit ratings |
| Rituals | The interest-rate announcement, quarterly earnings, budget day |
| Promise of salvation | "More growth, more wealth, more status — always more" |

- The distinguishing feature: **"It has no morals."**
- The GDP illustration: a car crash *raises* GDP (repairs, hospital bills, legal fees); a parent caring for a child at home "adds nothing"; a standing forest "counts for nothing," while the same forest "cut down and sold counts as growth."
- **The three temples:** BlackRock, Vanguard and State Street — asset managers that "don't make anything" and "don't sell you anything directly," but "manage other people's money, your pension very likely among it."
- Claimed scale: BlackRock "looks after more than 15 trillion dollars, more than the yearly output of every country on Earth except America and China."
- Concentration claim: "By one academic count," together these three are "the largest shareholder in nearly nine out of 10 of America's biggest companies" — including "rival airlines, rival banks, rival drug makers, the same owners on both sides of the competition."
- The ESG episode is used as proof of amorality: the firms "spoke the language of morality… climate pledges, social responsibility" until "it began to cost them"; then in **2025 the largest of them quietly walked away from its climate alliance.** "The religion without morals had learned to speak like one, for exactly as long as it paid."
- Core indictment: the system "doesn't ask whether something is good, only whether it is profitable… We argue about how to divide the money. We rarely ask what the money has made of us."

### 4. Layer three: the filter inside our heads

- "Every society has a set of things it agrees not to think about."
- Most people believe they see the world as it is; in reality they see it through "a shared set of moral and political assumptions we absorbed long before we could question them."
- The filter is comfortable — it assigns good and bad people and "spares us from thoughts that would cost us friends, jobs, or status" — but it leaves us disoriented because "some questions simply stop being asked."
- **Deliberately balanced examples of unaskable questions**, with the explicit point that agreement is not required:
  - Growth on a finite planet (taboo in one tribe).
  - Falling birth rates and rapid migration (taboo in the other).
  - Whether more money means better schools; whether markets judge a person's worth.
- The rule: "when a question becomes unaskable, the problem it points to doesn't disappear. It just carries on unexamined."
- Beyond the filter, power "doesn't mainly speak the language of rights and representation. It speaks the language of logistics, capital, demography, technology, and time."

### 5. Structure is not the enemy — society as an organism

- Pre-empting the obvious misreading: tearing down all hierarchy is "a fantasy." A village of a hundred can run on trust and conversation; "a nation of 60 million cannot" — it needs roads, courts, power grids, supply chains, "someone deciding where the water goes." At scale, "hierarchy and coordination aren't optional. They're how people stay alive."
- The nested scale: atoms → cells → organs and a brain → society.
- At that level, "society starts to behave like a living thing in its own right," with:
  - **A memory** — institutions and laws.
  - **An immune system** — how it "isolates people and ideas it sees as threats."
  - **Survival instincts no single person chose.**
- Therefore: "We are not just individuals living inside systems. We are cells inside a larger organism — and that organism is evolving."
- The reframed question: not whether power and structure should exist (they will), but "what kind of structure, accountable to whom, and moving in which direction?"

### 6. The clock: snapshots versus generations

- Human attention spans: news cycle (a day), social feed (a scroll), election cycle (4–5 years), career (40 years).
- Institutional lifespans: the **Bank of England has existed since 1694** and has "outlived dozens of governments and every one of their arguments"; **Norway's sovereign wealth fund is built to serve citizens who haven't been born yet.**
- "Institutions, capital, demographics, and technology all move on timescales of 50 to 200 years — longer than any single human life."
- The asymmetry, in the monologue's most quoted formulation: **"If you think in weeks, and the forces around you think in generations, you will always be reacting, never steering. You'll feel every wave and never see the tide."**

### 7. Two accelerating currents

**Demography**

- Japan's population "has been shrinking for over a decade."
- South Korea's birth rate "has fallen below one child per woman, among the lowest in the world."
- "Across much of Europe, the median person is now well into their 40s" — while "the median person in Nigeria is under 20."
- "By the middle of this century, roughly one in four people on Earth is projected to be African."
- Consequence: this "will reshape economies, borders, cultures, and the balance of global power," yet "much of our politics still talks as if the population of 30 years ago still exists."

**Artificial intelligence**

- Not "another tool, like a better spreadsheet" but **a force multiplier** that "amplifies whoever already controls the data, the computing power, and the institutions."
- Advanced systems are "built by a handful of companies and governments."
- It "compresses decisions that once took committees months into seconds"; can "automate persuasion at a scale no propaganda ministry ever dreamed of"; and "widens the gap between people who understand systems and people who only feel their effects."
- Combined with demographic shift: "the luxury of slow adaptation is gone."

### 8. The substrate: belief

- Asked what holds everything together, the answer is "not force, not laws, not even money. Belief."
- The £20 note: "plastic and ink," valuable only because "millions of people believe it is… If everyone stopped believing tomorrow morning, it would be plastic again by lunchtime."
- Nations, companies, borders and laws "none of them exist in nature. They exist because enough minds agree that they do." Hence: "The operating system was never written in code. It's written in shared belief."
- The personal chain: **belief decides what you notice → what you notice decides what you do → what you do, repeated, becomes who you are → who you are becomes your future.** ("Not by magic — by mechanism.")
- The placebo example: a sugar pill described as medicine makes many people "genuinely feel better."
- Because belief is the substrate, "so many forces are competing to decide what you believe":
  - The advert sells not a product but "a belief that you are not enough without it."
  - The feed installs "a belief that the other half of your country is your enemy."
  - The deepest installed belief: **that you are powerless** — "because a person who believes they are powerless doesn't need to be controlled. They control themselves."

### 9. So who is in control?

- "No one had to plan this. There's no secret room, no master plan."
- Answer: "In a sense, all of us" — the organism is steered by "what its cells believe together… a kind of mind that no single person owns," made of billions of private convictions "all pulling the same way without anyone choosing it."
- But: **"some cells sit closer to the controls than others."** Some are raised "taught from childhood that they are different — separate schools, separate lives — never once wondering how the rent gets paid."
- The structural critique is about distance, not malice: "It's hard to feel for people you never meet. So many of the decisions that shape our lives are made by people who will never feel their effects."
- **"They aren't villains. They're running on a belief they inherited, just like the rest of us. The difference is theirs is the belief that steers."**
- This is presented as the video's most hopeful point: with no villain, change is possible "one belief at a time. Starting with yours."

### 10. Closing prescription

Three questions to ask of any belief before accepting it:

1. **Who planted this?**
2. **Who benefits if I believe it?**
3. **Does it make my life larger or smaller?**

- Historical framing: "Every empire, every religion, every revolution in history began the same way — as a belief held by a few minds that spread until the organism itself changed shape."
- Final line: "The future is still being written. And it's being written in belief. Yours included. So be careful what you believe. One day, you'll be living inside it."
