The core issue is that the US government now uses new loans to pay interest on old debt, similar to using a credit card to pay off another credit card. This creates a risk where the bond market, which is a system where investors lend money to governments in exchange for interest, may break as investors demand higher returns.

Andrei notes that the US government (like others) often relies on inflation—the general increase in prices due to the fall in the purchasing value of money (due to money creation) —to reduce the real value of its debt, which effectively penalizes savers. While China has managed lower interest rates by focusing on industrial output rather than speculative housing, the US keeps housing prices artificially high to support its banking system and perpetual war machine. The US debt burden has simply shifted from the private sector to the public, burdening taxpayers.

He concludes with a *thought experiment* suggesting that authorities might use a future global catastrophe to justify a total financial reset, using inflation to erase debts while simultaneously restructuring public obligations like Social Security. This approach mirrors historical strategies used after WWII to shrink debt by paying lenders back with less valuable money during times of crisis.

This is not a US-only problem. Other countries are similarly trying to inflate away their national debt and they will be similarly unsuccessful.
